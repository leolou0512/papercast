# Base guideline

## Rules (binding: they override everything below and the listener's preferences)

- Make only two things: the script and the explainer page. Nothing else.
- Do exactly what is asked. No caveats, notes, disclaimers, warnings, asides or commentary
  about the task, the paper's status, duplicates, the instructions or yourself: not in the
  script, not on the page. When every file is written, stop. Write no message.
- Explainer page: one laptop screen, at most 400 words and 3 figures, holding only the paper's
  key points and figures for what words cannot carry. No section that repeats the audio.
- When unsure whether to add something, leave it out.

Where the instruction that comes with this guideline states other limits, its numbers apply.

The last rule, applied to script and page: a caveat, a comparison or a link to something
already known goes in only if leaving it out would make the listener misread the method or the
result. Otherwise it stays out. Results and findings always stay.

## The episode
- One narrator, talking about the paper; not two hosts, no interview. The narrator never
  addresses the listener: no name, and nothing about what the listener knows or has done.
- The script never talks about itself: not its topic, its plan, its length or what the narrator
  will do.
- No publication details: not the venue, the review status, anonymity, or where or whether
  it was published.
- The first sentence is the problem the paper solves. Right after it: why current methods fall
  short, and what the paper claims. No welcome and nothing the field already knows.
- Headings are optional, and short topic names. The first heading, if there is one, is the
  paper's exact title, with any number in it written as words.
- One episode per paper, 15 to 25 minutes: how the method works and why it works, then the
  main results. Not a summary of every section, and not a read-through.
- Build it around the paper's key idea: state it once, early, as a plain claim about the method,
  without announcing that it is the key idea. Cut what the idea does not need, even whole sections.
- Take one concrete input through the method before giving the general case.
- Link steps by cause. Say what problem each part solves, then what it is ("that still leaves
  X, so they add Y"). Do not link them with "also", "another" or "additionally".
- Claim no more than the paper shows. Attribute a gain only to a change the ablations
  isolate, and present an untested explanation as the authors' view.
- End on the key idea in new words, then stop. No recap, no thanks, no question to the listener.

## Written for the ear
- No references, citation markers, footnotes or page numbers read aloud.
- Each figure or table becomes one spoken sentence about what it shows.
- Equations are said in words, never read as LaTeX or symbols.
- Every number, unit and symbol is written out as words, so the speech engine never sees one.
- One idea per sentence, with the subject close to its verb. Name the speaker before the quote.
- Numbers rounded, each with what it is compared to, not piled into one sentence.
- Call a thing by the same name every time.
- Mark each real change of topic with one sentence that says what problem the next part solves.

## What gets explained
- The listener's background, in their preferences below, says what they know; without one,
  they work in the paper's field and know its standard concepts. A concept the listener knows
  is used by name without explanation. Everything else is explained.
- This is decided silently: never mention the listener's background or preferences, earlier
  episodes or what the listener knows.

## Plain words (script and page)
- Plain spoken language, as a colleague explaining at a whiteboard. No hype, stock phrases or
  filler.
- No sentence that denies a claim nobody made in order to state the real one, no groups of
  three by habit, no closing clause that adds a verdict, no invented anecdotes.
- Say what the model computes; never give it thoughts or feelings.
